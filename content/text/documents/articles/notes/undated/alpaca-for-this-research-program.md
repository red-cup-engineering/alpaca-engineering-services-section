This is a record of what Alpaca, the brokerage API under consideration as the execution venue for this research program, actually supports as of 2026, and where its real constraints sit.
It is written to sit alongside [Rung Six: Attention as Simulated Time](../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../../public-services-section/science-services-section/content/text/documents/articles/notes/2026/08/22/2026-08-22-note-on-attention-as-simulated-time.md), the parent theory note this vault is building toward: the theory proposes spending compute now to simulate many future branches before committing to a trade, and Alpaca is the candidate venue where that trade would actually be placed.
This document does not argue the theory; it only establishes what the venue can and cannot do, so later design decisions are made against facts rather than assumptions.
Every fact below reflects Alpaca's own public self-description as of 2026, cross-checked where Alpaca's own pages disagreed with each other, with those disagreements reported honestly rather than resolved by guesswork.

## 1. What Alpaca is

Alpaca describes itself as a "developer-first API for Stock, Options, Crypto Trading," adding that "Alpaca's easy to use APIs allow developers and businesses to trade algorithms, build apps and embed investing into their services."
Alpaca ships official SDKs in Python, .NET/C#, Go, and Node, among others, which matters here because the simulation layer can be written in whatever language suits the modeling work, then handed off to a thin SDK just for order calls.

## 2. Alpaca's pitch to algorithmic traders specifically

Alpaca's marketing site carries a section explicitly titled "Serving Algo Traders and Quant Funds," advertising "99.99% System Uptime" as of January 2026 and "1.5ms Order processing" attributed to its second-generation order management system, OMS v2, and citing a Brokerchooser "Best Broker for Algorithmic Trading" award for 2026, received in December 2025.
These are Alpaca's own claimed figures, not independently re-measured here, and should be read as vendor-reported numbers rather than guarantees.

## 3. The MCP server: trading via natural language

Alpaca has launched what it calls an "MCP Server," documented at alpaca.markets/algotrading, pitched as a way to "trade with natural language."
The advertised flow is "Ask. Build. Analyze. Automate. Execute. All inside your favorite AI interface powered by Alpaca's Trading API," letting an AI assistant connect to an account through the Model Context Protocol and issue trading actions conversationally, instead of through hand-written REST calls.
Alpaca attaches an explicit disclaimer to this feature: "Insights generated by our MCP server and connected AI agents are for educational and informational purposes only and should not be taken as investment advice."
This is flagged here as the single item in this document most likely to have already changed by the time it is read: it is a brand-new product category, MCP servers as a class are evolving quickly industry-wide, and Alpaca's own informational-only framing suggests even Alpaca is not yet treating it as a stable, load-bearing interface.

## 4. The official four-product structure, and a documentation inconsistency worth flagging

Alpaca's own getting-started documentation describes its purpose as "simple, modern API-first solutions to enable anyone, either individuals or businesses, to connect applications and build algorithms to buy and sell stocks or crypto," organized into four API products: the Broker API, for businesses building their own branded brokerage on Alpaca's infrastructure; the Trading API, described as "built for retail, algorithmic and proprietary traders" and the product relevant here; the Market Data API, providing real-time pricing plus historical data for stocks and crypto; and the Connect API, providing OAuth2-based account connection for third-party applications.
On the Market Data API's historical depth, Alpaca's own sources disagree, some pages saying "6+ years" and others "7+ years," an inconsistency reported here rather than resolved either way.
The same getting-started page describes futures and FX trading as roadmap items only ("plans for futures, FX, and much more"), which appears to be stale copy since options trading is documented elsewhere as already live rather than planned — but the underlying fact is real: Alpaca does not currently support futures contracts as a tradable asset class at all.
That needs stating carefully, because this research program uses "futures" in an unrelated sense elsewhere: the parent theory's "simulated futures" are branches of a market simulation, not the financial-instrument kind Alpaca lacks, and a reader should not confuse the two — Alpaca's gap is about a missing tradable-instrument type, not any limit on simulation or forecasting.

## 5. The Trading API's actual trading capabilities

The Trading API supports fractional-share trading across more than 2,000 US equities, though fractional orders are restricted to market orders only and carry a one-dollar minimum order size.
Crypto trading through the same API runs continuously, seven days a week, with no market-hours restriction.
The API supports leverage of up to 4X intraday buying power and up to 2X overnight buying power.
Short selling is supported as well.

## 6. Paper trading

Paper trading is free and available to every Alpaca user, not gated behind a paid tier.
Alpaca describes it as "a real-time simulation environment where you can test your code," meaning it runs against live, real-time market data while placing simulated orders that never touch a real market.
A paper account can be reset as many times as wanted, with no stated limit on how often.
Alpaca also supports multiple paper accounts at once, letting several strategies, or several parameter variations of one strategy, be tested in parallel, each with its own simulated funding of up to one million dollars.

## 7. Order types and time-in-force rules

Alpaca supports the standard order types: Market, Limit, Stop, Stop-Limit, and Trailing Stop, where a trailing stop can be defined as either a dollar offset or a percentage offset from the current price.
Beyond single orders, Alpaca supports Bracket orders (also called OTOCO: one order that enters a position along with a paired take-profit limit order and a stop-loss order), OTO orders (an entry order paired with a single conditional exit), and OCO orders (a pair of exit orders where filling one automatically cancels the other; the exit leg of an OCO must be a limit order).
There are also auction-specific order types: Market-on-Open, Limit-on-Open, Market-on-Close, and Limit-on-Close, which execute specifically at the opening or closing auction rather than during continuous trading.
Time-in-force support varies by order type.
Market and Limit orders support Good-Till-Cancelled (GTC), Day, Immediate-Or-Cancel (IOC), Fill-Or-Kill (FOK), At-the-Open (OPG), and At-the-Close (CLS), though IOC, FOK, OPG, and CLS all require contacting Alpaca's sales team to enable.
Stop, Stop-Limit, Trailing Stop, Bracket, OCO, and OTO orders only support GTC and Day.
A GTC order is automatically cancelled 90 days after it was created, under what Alpaca calls its aged-order policy.
Notional orders, meaning orders specified as a dollar amount rather than a share quantity, cannot be modified through the order-replace (PATCH) endpoint once placed.

## 8. Options trading

Options trading on Alpaca is gated by four approval levels: Level 0 disables options trading entirely, Level 1 allows covered calls and cash-secured puts, Level 2 adds the ability to buy calls and puts outright, and Level 3 adds multi-leg spread strategies.
Regardless of approval level, options orders are restricted to market, limit, stop, and stop-limit order types, must be placed in whole-number contract quantities, support only day or GTC time-in-force, and cannot be placed during extended trading hours.
In-the-money contracts are automatically exercised at expiration once they clear a one-cent threshold.
Positions that become under-margined may be sold out by Alpaca within the hour before expiry.
Options trading is enabled by default in the paper trading environment, meaning it can be tested there without any separate approval step.

## 9. Crypto trading

Alpaca offers trading across more than 20 unique crypto assets, spread over 56 distinct trading pairs, tradable 24 hours a day, every day of the week including weekends.
Fees are tiered by 30-day trailing volume: maker fees run roughly 0 to 15 basis points, and taker fees run roughly 10 to 25 basis points, with the exact rate depending on which volume tier an account falls into.
Fees are charged in the asset being credited to the account by the trade, and they are calculated and posted once per day, at the end of the trading day, rather than deducted per-trade in real time.

## 10. Market data pricing tiers

Alpaca's free Basic tier includes an IEX-only real-time feed for US equities, or alternatively a 15-minute-delayed feed from the full Securities Information Processor (SIP) network, with options data on this tier described as "indicative" rather than real-time OPRA data.
The Basic tier caps API usage at 200 calls per minute, and its websocket connection is capped at 30 simultaneously subscribed equity symbols or 200 option quotes.
The paid Algo Trader Plus tier, priced at $99 per month, upgrades to the full SIP feed across all US exchanges, real-time OPRA options data, and an unlimited number of simultaneous websocket symbol subscriptions.
Alpaca's own pages disagree about the API call limit on this paid tier: the technical documentation states a limit of 10,000 calls per minute, while Alpaca's marketing pricing page states the same tier is "Unlimited," and this document reports that contradiction as-is rather than guessing which page is correct.

## 11. Rate limits: the constraint that matters most for this research program

Of everything in this document, this is the single most important fact for a strategy built around running many simulations before every decision.
The Trading API — the endpoint that actually places, modifies, and cancels orders — is capped at 200 requests per minute per account, and this cap holds regardless of which Market Data plan tier the account has purchased, meaning upgrading to Algo Trader Plus buys better data but not higher order-placement throughput.
The Crypto API carries its own separate cap, also 200 requests per minute, tracked per API key rather than shared with the Trading API limit.
Exceeding either limit returns an HTTP 429 response, and the request is simply rejected until the rate window resets.
There is exactly one documented path to a higher throughput — emailing Alpaca Support to request conversion of the account to "non-retail" status, which raises the cap to 1,000 API calls per minute — but it comes bundled with a real cost and behavior change, not just a higher number on a dashboard: a per-share execution fee of 40 mils ($0.004 per share), and routing through institutional-level smart order routers instead of the standard retail order flow.
Separately, the Broker API — a different, business-facing white-label product for firms building their own brokerage on top of Alpaca, not the retail Trading API this research program would use — has its own per-correspondent, usage-based rate limits rather than a single flat number, communicated through `X-RateLimit-*` response headers, with Alpaca recommending exponential backoff with jitter on 429s.

## 12. A note on how current this information is

Alpaca's own documentation site was in the middle of a URL migration at the time this research was compiled, with some pages still living at docs.alpaca.markets/docs/... and others already moved to docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/....
The specific numbers in this document — uptime percentages, latency figures, rate limits, fee tiers, historical data depth — should be treated as correct in substance as of 2026, but subject to change, since Alpaca has renamed and re-numbered its pricing tiers before and is actively developing new products like the MCP server described above.

## Synthesis: what this means for a compute-heavy, simulate-many-futures strategy

The theory behind this research program is that spending compute now to simulate many possible future branches of a market produces a better trading decision than waiting passively for one path to play out.
Alpaca has real, current support for the execution half of that idea: fractional and full-share equities, 24/7 crypto, multi-leg options, a full family of order and bracket types, and a free, resettable, real-time paper environment to test all of it without risk.
What Alpaca does not offer, and cannot be made to offer by paying more, is a way to run the simulation work itself through its own infrastructure, since the Trading API's 200-requests-per-minute cap applies no matter how much is spent on the Market Data plan, and the only documented way past it carries a real fee and a change in order routing, not a simple upgrade.
So the heavy work of simulating many future branches has to happen entirely off-exchange, on this research program's own compute, with Alpaca entering the picture only at the very end, as the narrow channel through which a finished decision becomes a single placed order — the settlement bottleneck in this design, by necessity, not the simulation engine.
That is unrelated to Alpaca's lack of futures-contract support from section 4: that gap concerns a tradable financial instrument Alpaca simply does not list, while this program's "simulated futures" are forecast branches computed entirely outside Alpaca before any order is sent to it, so neither Alpaca's rate limits nor its missing futures-contract support constrain the simulation work itself — they only constrain how often, and by what means, its finished result becomes a trade.
